What will my loan really cost? Monthly payments for common loan sizes, how interest is calculated, and how much you can borrow

Short answer: a $10,000 loan at 8% over 5 years costs about $203 a month and $2,166 in total interest. Over 3 years the payment rises to $313 a month, but you’d pay only $1,281 in interest. A shorter term means bigger payments but a much cheaper loan.

Whether it’s a personal loan, a car loan or consolidating debt, the two numbers that matter are the monthly payment (can I afford it?) and the total interest (what am I really paying?). How loan interest is calculated

Each month, interest is charged on what you still owe: balance × (annual rate ÷ 12). Your payment covers that interest first, and the rest reduces the balance. That’s why early payments are mostly interest and later ones mostly principal – and why paying a little extra early on saves the most.

The payment formula is M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where P is the loan amount, r the monthly rate and n the number of payments.

How much can I borrow?

Lenders mostly look at your income, your existing debts and your credit history. A common yardstick is the debt-to-income ratio (DTI): many lenders like total monthly debt payments – including the new loan – to stay below about 36% of your gross monthly income, though some accept more. On $5,000 a month gross, that’s about $1,800 for all debts. Our debt-to-income calculator shows where you stand.

Questions people ask about loans

How much loan can I afford?
Work backwards from a comfortable monthly payment. If $300 a month fits your budget, at 8% over 5 years that supports a loan of about $14,800. Leave room for savings and emergencies rather than borrowing the maximum a lender offers.
Is APR the same as the interest rate?
Not quite. The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the money; the APR adds most fees (like an origination fee) and spreads them over the loan, so it’s the better number for comparing offers. A 7% loan with a 3% fee has an APR of about 8.3% over 5 years.
Do loans affect my credit score?
Applying usually causes a small, temporary dip from the credit check. Paying on time every month builds your score over time; missed payments hurt it significantly.
Is it better to take a shorter loan term?
If you can comfortably afford the higher payment, yes – you’ll pay much less interest. If the payment would stretch you, a longer term with the option to overpay gives flexibility; check there’s no early repayment fee.
Is debt consolidation a good idea?
It can be, if the new loan’s APR is lower than what you’re paying now and you don’t run the old cards back up. Compare the total cost, not just the lower monthly payment – stretching debt over more years can cost more overall.
