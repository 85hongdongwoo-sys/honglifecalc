Debt-to-capital ratio

Debt-to-capital ratio: what counts as debt, how to calculate it, and how lenders and analysts read it

Short answer: debt-to-capital = interest-bearing debt ÷ (debt + shareholders’ equity). A company with $300,000 of loans and bonds and $500,000 of equity has a ratio of 0.375 (37.5%) – just over a third of its long-term funding is borrowed.

Every company funds itself with a mix of borrowed money and owners’ money. The debt-to-capital ratio shows that mix in one number. Unlike the debt-to-asset ratio, it focuses on financing – loans, bonds and leases – and leaves out everyday operating liabilities like supplier invoices.

What goes into “debt”

  • Included: bank loans, overdrafts, bonds and notes, and (under current accounting rules) lease liabilities.
  • Usually excluded: accounts payable, accrued wages, deferred revenue – liabilities that don’t carry interest.

Questions people ask

What is a good debt-to-capital ratio?
There’s no single answer – it depends on the industry and how stable the company’s cash flow is. Many analysts treat anything well above 50–60% as a sign to look closely at interest cover and refinancing risk.
Should equity be book value or market value?
Book value is standard in financial statements and loan covenants. Analysts valuing a company often use market value instead, which gives a different (often lower) ratio for profitable firms.
How does it differ from debt-to-asset?
Debt-to-asset divides all liabilities by total assets. Debt-to-capital divides only interest-bearing debt by debt plus equity. See the debt-to-asset calculator.
