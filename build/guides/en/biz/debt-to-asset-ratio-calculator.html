Debt-to-asset ratio

Debt-to-asset ratio: how to calculate it, what a “good” ratio is, and how it compares with debt-to-equity

Short answer: debt-to-asset ratio = total liabilities ÷ total assets. A company with $1,000,000 of assets and $400,000 of debts has a ratio of 0.40 – 40% of its assets are financed by creditors and 60% by owners. Below 0.5 is generally seen as conservative, but norms vary a lot by industry.

This ratio answers a simple question about financial risk: if the business had to pay off everything it owes, how much of its assets would that use up? Banks look at it when deciding on loans, and investors use it to compare how leveraged companies are.

Reading the ratio

RatioInterpretation
Under 0.3Low leverage – mostly financed by owners’ money
0.3 – 0.6Moderate – common for established businesses
Above 0.6Highly leveraged – more sensitive to falling sales or rising interest rates
Above 1.0Liabilities exceed assets – negative equity

Capital-heavy and regulated sectors (utilities, banks, property) normally run higher ratios than software or consulting firms.

Related ratios

  • Debt-to-equity = liabilities ÷ equity. A debt-to-asset ratio of 0.40 equals a debt-to-equity of 0.67.
  • Debt-to-capital = interest-bearing debt ÷ (debt + equity) – it leaves out non-interest liabilities like supplier bills. See the debt-to-capital calculator.
  • Equity ratio = equity ÷ assets = 1 − debt-to-asset ratio.

Questions people ask

What is a good debt-to-asset ratio?
For many non-financial companies, around 0.3 to 0.6 is typical. Lower means less risk but possibly slower growth; higher can boost returns in good years and hurt badly in bad ones.
Should I use total liabilities or only debt?
The standard ratio uses total liabilities. Some analysts use only interest-bearing debt – state which version you use so comparisons are fair.
Can I use this for personal finances?
Yes. Divide everything you owe (mortgage, car loan, cards) by everything you own. A falling ratio over the years is a good sign of growing net worth.
