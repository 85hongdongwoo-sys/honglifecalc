Income elasticity of demand

Income elasticity of demand: the formula, how to tell normal, luxury and inferior goods apart, and a worked example

Short answer: income elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income. If household income rises from $50,000 to $55,000 and restaurant meals go from 200 to 230 a year, the midpoint elasticity is about 1.47 – above 1, so restaurant meals behave like a luxury good here.

Income elasticity shows what happens to demand when people get richer or poorer. Businesses use it to predict how sales will move through a boom or recession; students meet it as one of the standard elasticity measures.

Types of goods by income elasticity

ElasticityType of goodExample
Above 1Luxury (income-elastic)Overseas holidays, dining out, designer clothes
Between 0 and 1Necessity (normal, inelastic)Basic groceries, electricity, toothpaste
Below 0Inferior goodInstant noodles, cheapest own-brand items, long-distance coaches

Examples are typical tendencies; actual elasticities depend on the country, the income level and the time period studied.

The worked example, step by step

  1. % change in quantity (midpoint) = 30 ÷ 215 = 13.95%
  2. % change in income (midpoint) = 5,000 ÷ 52,500 = 9.52%
  3. Income elasticity = 13.95 ÷ 9.52 ≈ 1.47

Questions people ask

Can income elasticity be negative?
Yes – that’s the definition of an inferior good: people buy less of it as their income rises, usually because they switch to something they prefer.
Why does income elasticity matter for business?
Sellers of luxury goods see sales swing strongly with the economy, while sellers of necessities are more stable. Knowing which you are helps with planning stock, staff and prices.
How is it different from price elasticity?
Price elasticity looks at the response to a product’s own price; income elasticity looks at the response to buyers’ income. See the price elasticity calculator.
