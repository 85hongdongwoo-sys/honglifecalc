Price elasticity of demand

How to calculate price elasticity of demand with the midpoint formula – and what it tells you about raising or cutting prices

Short answer: elasticity = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price. If raising the price from $10 to $12 cuts sales from 1,000 to 850 units, the midpoint elasticity is about −0.89. Because it’s between 0 and −1, demand is inelastic – and indeed revenue rises from $10,000 to $10,200.

Elasticity measures how sensitive buyers are to price. It’s a core idea in economics classes, and a practical one for anyone setting prices: it tells you whether a price rise will bring in more money or drive customers away.

The midpoint (arc) formula, step by step

  1. % change in quantity = (850 − 1,000) ÷ average of 1,000 and 850 (925) = −16.2%
  2. % change in price = (12 − 10) ÷ average of 10 and 12 (11) = +18.2%
  3. Elasticity = −16.2% ÷ 18.2% ≈ −0.89

Using averages makes the answer the same whether the price goes up or down – that’s why textbooks prefer the midpoint method.

Questions people ask about price elasticity

Why is price elasticity usually negative?
Because price and quantity demanded normally move in opposite directions – higher price, fewer sales. Economists often quote the absolute value (0.89 rather than −0.89).
What makes demand elastic or inelastic?
Demand is more elastic when there are close substitutes, when the item is a luxury or a big share of the budget, and over longer time periods. Necessities with few substitutes – like petrol in the short run or essential medicines – tend to be inelastic.
What’s the difference between point and arc elasticity?
Point elasticity uses a derivative at one price on the demand curve; arc (midpoint) elasticity uses two observed price–quantity pairs, which is what you usually have in real data.
How is this different from income elasticity?
Income elasticity measures how demand responds to changes in buyers’ income rather than price. Try the income elasticity calculator.
