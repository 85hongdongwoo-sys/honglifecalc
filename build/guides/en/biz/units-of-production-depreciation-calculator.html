Units-of-production depreciation

Units-of-production depreciation: the formula, a machine example, and when it beats straight-line

Short answer: depreciation per unit = (cost − salvage value) ÷ total expected units. A $50,000 machine with a $5,000 salvage value that should make 100,000 units depreciates $0.45 per unit. If it makes 18,000 units this year, this year’s depreciation is $8,100.

Most equipment doesn’t wear out with the calendar – it wears out with use. The units-of-production method ties the expense to how hard an asset actually worked, so busy years carry more depreciation and quiet years less.

When to use it

  • Machinery, vehicles (per mile or km), printing presses, mining and oil assets (per tonne or barrel).
  • When usage varies a lot from year to year, so straight-line would mismatch costs and revenue.
  • It needs a reliable usage record and a realistic estimate of total lifetime units.

Questions people ask

Is units-of-production allowed for tax?
It’s an accepted method for financial reporting under GAAP and IFRS. Tax rules differ by country – in the US, most business property uses MACRS for tax, so a separate tax schedule is common.
What if the machine makes more units than expected?
Once book value reaches salvage value, depreciation stops. If estimates were clearly wrong, accountants revise the rate going forward rather than restating past years.
How is it different from straight-line?
Straight-line spreads the cost evenly over time – here $9,000 a year for five years – regardless of usage. Units-of-production moves with actual output.
