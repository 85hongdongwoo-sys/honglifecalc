ROAS for advertisers

What is a good ROAS? How to calculate return on ad spend, find your break-even ROAS, and avoid the profit trap

Short answer: ROAS = revenue from ads ÷ ad spend. Spend $1,000 and make $5,000 in sales and your ROAS is 5 (or 500%). Whether that’s good depends on your margin: your break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ profit margin. With a 40% margin you need at least 2.5 just to cover the ads.

ROAS is the number every ad dashboard shows first, and the one that’s easiest to misread. A ROAS of 3 can be a great result for one business and a loss-maker for another. The key is to compare it with what your margins actually allow.

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin. Above it, the ads add profit; below it, each sale loses money after paying for the ads.

Worked example

  1. You spend $2,000 on ads and they bring in $7,000 of sales: ROAS = 7,000 ÷ 2,000 = 3.5.
  2. Your gross margin is 35%, so the sales leave $2,450 of gross profit.
  3. Subtract the ad spend: $2,450 − $2,000 = $450 profit from the campaign.
  4. Break-even ROAS for you is 1 ÷ 0.35 ≈ 2.9, so 3.5 is comfortably above it.

Questions people ask about ROAS

What is a good ROAS?
Anything above your break-even ROAS makes money, and many businesses aim for a buffer on top to cover overheads – often 3–4 for e-commerce. But a lower ROAS can still be worth it if new customers come back and buy again (high lifetime value).
Is ROAS the same as ROI?
No. ROAS only compares revenue with ad spend. ROI compares profit with the total cost, including product costs, fees and staff time. A campaign can have a healthy ROAS and a negative ROI.
How do I express ROAS – as a ratio or a percentage?
Both are used: a ROAS of 4 is the same as 400% or 4:1. Ad platforms usually show the ratio.
How is ROAS measured in Google Ads or Meta?
The platform divides the conversion value it attributes to your ads by the cost. Attribution windows and tracking gaps mean platform ROAS often differs from what your shop records, so check it against real sales.
