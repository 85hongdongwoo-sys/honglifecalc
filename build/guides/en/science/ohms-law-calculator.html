Ohm’s law in practice

Ohm’s law made simple – V = I × R, the power formulas, and real examples from LEDs to kettles

The rule: voltage equals current times resistance, V = I × R. Rearranged, I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I. A 12 V supply across a 4 Ω resistor drives 3 A, and that resistor turns 12 × 3 = 36 W into heat.

Ohm’s law is the first formula most people learn in electronics, and it stays useful forever: choosing a resistor for an LED, checking whether a wire will get warm, or working out why a fuse blew. Knowing any two of voltage, current and resistance gives you the third – and with power (P = V × I) you can find all four.

Real-world examples

  • LED resistor: a red LED drops about 2 V and wants 20 mA. From a 5 V supply: R = (5 − 2) ÷ 0.02 = 150 Ω. Use the next standard value up (150 Ω exists; otherwise 180 Ω).
  • Kettle: a 2,300 W kettle on 230 V mains draws 2,300 ÷ 230 = 10 A – fine on a 13 A UK plug fuse. On 120 V in the US, a 1,500 W kettle draws 12.5 A.
  • Resistor rating: 9 V across 100 Ω gives 90 mA and 0.81 W – too much for a common ¼ W resistor; pick a 1 W part.
  • Phone charger: 5 V at 2 A is 10 W; a 20 W USB-C charger usually supplies 9 V at about 2.2 A.
Mains electricity (120 V / 230 V) can kill. Use these formulas for understanding and low-voltage projects; leave work on household wiring to a qualified electrician.

Questions people ask about Ohm’s law

Does Ohm’s law always apply?
It holds well for ordinary resistors and wires at a steady temperature. Components like LEDs, diodes, light bulbs (whose resistance rises when hot) and transistors are “non-ohmic” – their current doesn’t rise in a straight line with voltage.
What happens if I double the voltage?
With the same resistance, the current doubles and the power quadruples (P = V² ÷ R). That’s why a component rated for 5 V can burn out quickly on 10 V.
How do I calculate resistance from voltage and current?
Divide voltage by current: R = V ÷ I. If a heater draws 4 A at 120 V, its resistance is 30 Ω.
What are the units?
Voltage in volts (V), current in amperes (A), resistance in ohms (Ω) and power in watts (W). Remember to convert milliamps to amps (20 mA = 0.02 A) before using the formula.
Is Ohm’s law the same for AC?
For purely resistive loads like heaters, yes, using RMS values. Motors and electronics add reactance, so engineers use impedance (Z) instead of plain resistance.
