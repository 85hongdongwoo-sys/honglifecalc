Percentages, made painless

How to work out percentages in your head – discounts, tips, pay rises and the classic traps

The one rule to remember: “percent” means “out of 100”, so X% of a number = number × X ÷ 100. 20% of 85 is 85 × 0.20 = 17. Every other percentage question – increase, decrease, “what percent is”, reverse percentages – is a small twist on that rule.

Percentages turn up everywhere: the sale sign in a shop window, a pay rise letter, a tip at dinner, a test score. The calculator above does the arithmetic instantly, but it helps to know the three questions you’re really asking, because most mistakes come from mixing them up.

Mental shortcuts that actually work

  • 10%: move the decimal point one place left. 10% of 63 = 6.3.
  • 5%: half of 10%. 5% of 63 = 3.15.
  • 15% or 20% tip: 10% plus half of it (15%), or 10% doubled (20%). On a $48 bill: 15% ≈ $7.20, 20% ≈ $9.60.
  • 1%: move the decimal two places. Useful for odd numbers – 17% is 10% + 5% + 2 × 1%.
  • Swap trick: X% of Y is the same as Y% of X. 8% of 50 is hard; 50% of 8 is 4.

Everyday examples

  • Sale price: 30% off $80 → you pay 70% → 80 × 0.70 = $56.
  • Two discounts stacked (20% then an extra 10%): 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72, so it’s 28% off in total – not 30%.
  • Pay rise: from $52,000 to $55,000 is 3,000 ÷ 52,000 = 5.8%.
  • Original price before a discount (reverse percentage): paid $63 after 10% off → 63 ÷ 0.90 = $70.
  • Removing sales tax or VAT: price including 20% tax is $90 → 90 ÷ 1.20 = $75 before tax (not 90 − 20%).
  • Test score: 42 out of 50 → 42 ÷ 50 × 100 = 84%.

Questions people ask about percentages

How do I calculate a percentage of a number on a phone calculator?
Type the number, press ×, type the percentage, then ÷ 100 (or press % if your calculator has it). For 18% of 250: 250 × 18 ÷ 100 = 45.
How do I work out what percentage one number is of another?
Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. If 27 of 36 people said yes, that’s 27 ÷ 36 × 100 = 75%.
What’s the difference between percent and percentage points?
Percentage points are a plain difference between two percentages; percent is a relative change. If a rate goes from 4% to 5%, it rose by 1 percentage point – but that’s a 25% increase in the rate. News reports mix these up all the time.
How do I find the original number before a percentage was added?
Divide by (1 + the percentage as a decimal). If a price after a 25% markup is $150, the original was 150 ÷ 1.25 = $120. Taking 25% off $150 would give the wrong answer ($112.50).
Can a percentage be more than 100%?
Yes, when you compare something to a smaller starting value. If sales went from 200 to 500, that’s a 150% increase, and 500 is 250% of 200. Only a share of a whole (like “percent of the class”) is capped at 100%.
