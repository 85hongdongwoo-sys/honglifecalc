How interest-only loans work – the lower payment now, the bigger payment later, and when they make sense

Short answer: on a $300,000 loan at 7%, the interest-only payment is $1,750 a month (300,000 × 7% ÷ 12), versus about $1,996 for a normal 30-year repayment loan. But after a 10-year interest-only period, you still owe the full $300,000 – and repaying it over the remaining 20 years costs about $2,326 a month.

Interest-only loans let you pay just the interest for a set period, so the monthly payment is lower. They’re used for investment properties, bridging finance and by borrowers with irregular income who expect a big payout later. The catch is simple: nothing comes off the balance until the interest-only period ends.

When an interest-only loan can make sense

  • Buy-to-let investors who expect the property’s rent to cover interest and plan to repay from a sale later.
  • Bridging between buying a new home and selling the old one.
  • People with large, predictable future income such as bonuses or a business sale – with a clear repayment plan.
Lenders usually require proof of a credible “repayment vehicle” – savings, investments or a property sale – for the full balance. If house prices fall, you could owe more than the home is worth when the interest-only period ends.

Questions people ask about interest-only loans

How do I calculate an interest-only payment?
Multiply the balance by the annual rate and divide by 12. $250,000 at 6% is 250,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $1,250 a month.
Can I pay off principal during the interest-only period?
Usually yes, and it lowers the following interest payments. Check for overpayment limits or early repayment charges in your loan terms.
Is an interest-only mortgage cheaper?
Monthly, yes, at first. Overall, no – because the balance stays high for longer, you pay more interest over the life of the loan than with a standard repayment mortgage.
What happens at the end of the interest-only period?
Either the loan switches to repayment (and the payment jumps) or the whole balance becomes due. Plan for that moment well in advance – many borrowers refinance or sell.
