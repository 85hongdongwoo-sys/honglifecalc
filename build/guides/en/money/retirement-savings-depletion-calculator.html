Retirement drawdown

How long will my retirement savings last? Years of income from $1 million at different withdrawal rates – and how to make it stretch

Short answer: with $1 million, withdrawing $40,000 a year (raised 3% a year for inflation) and earning 5% a year, the money lasts about 33 years. Take $50,000 a year and it lasts about 24 years; $60,000 a year, about 19 years.

Running out of money is the biggest worry most retirees have, and it’s a fair one: people are living longer, and a retirement can easily last 25–35 years. The good news is that a few simple choices – how much you take out, how flexible you are, and how your money is invested – make a huge difference.

Ways to make savings last longer

  • Start with a lower withdrawal rate. Moving from 5% to 4% can add nearly a decade in the example above.
  • Be flexible in bad years. Skipping an inflation increase or trimming spending by 5–10% after a market fall protects the portfolio when it matters most.
  • Delay Social Security or the State Pension if you can – it raises a guaranteed, inflation-linked income for life.
  • Keep a cash buffer of one to two years’ spending so you don’t have to sell investments after a crash.
  • Mind fees and taxes. A 1% annual fee is a quarter of a 4% withdrawal.

Questions people ask about retirement savings

How long will $500,000 last in retirement?
Withdrawing $25,000 a year (5%) with the same assumptions, about 24 years; withdrawing $20,000 (4%), about 33 years. Halve the savings and the withdrawal, and the timeline stays the same.
What is the 4% rule?
A guideline from US research: withdraw 4% of your savings in the first year, then increase it with inflation. Historically it lasted at least 30 years in almost all periods studied. It’s a starting point, not a guarantee.
What return should I assume?
It depends on your mix of investments. A balanced portfolio might reasonably assume 4–6% a year before inflation; holding mostly cash or bonds means lower returns. Being cautious here is safer than being optimistic.
What is sequence-of-returns risk?
If markets fall sharply in your first few years of withdrawals, you sell more units at low prices and the portfolio may never fully recover – even if average returns over 30 years are fine. A cash buffer and flexible spending are the main defences.
