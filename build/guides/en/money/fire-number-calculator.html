What is my FIRE number? The 25× rule, 4% vs 3.5% withdrawals, and FIRE targets for common spending levels

Short answer: your FIRE number is roughly 25 times your yearly spending – the amount at which a 4% withdrawal covers your costs. Spend $40,000 a year and you’d aim for about $1 million. For a more cautious 3.5% withdrawal rate (better for early retirees), multiply by about 28.6 instead: around $1.14 million.

FIRE – Financial Independence, Retire Early – boils down to one question: how much do you need invested so that your portfolio can pay for your life indefinitely? The answer comes from your spending, not your income, which is why cutting costs speeds things up twice: you save more and you need less.

Where the 4% rule comes from – and its limits

The 4% figure traces back to US research in the 1990s (the Bengen study and the “Trinity study”) that looked at historical stock and bond returns. Withdrawing 4% of the starting balance, then adjusting for inflation, survived almost every 30-year period. Early retirees face 40–50 years, higher fees or a weak first decade, so many prefer 3.25–3.5% for a bigger safety margin – or plan to cut spending a little in bad years.

Ways to lower your FIRE number

  • Expected pensions or Social Security later in life reduce what the portfolio must cover after that age.
  • Part-time or “Barista FIRE” income of even $15,000 a year cuts the target by $375,000 at 4%.
  • Paying off the mortgage before you stop work lowers ongoing spending – but compare that with investing the money.
  • Health insurance is a big extra cost for early retirees in the US; budget for it explicitly.

Questions people ask about FIRE

Is $1 million enough to retire early?
At a 4% withdrawal it supports about $40,000 a year; at 3.5%, about $35,000. Whether that’s enough depends on your spending, where you live and whether you have other income or pensions coming later.
What is Coast FIRE?
The point where you’ve saved enough that, with no more contributions, growth alone would reach your FIRE number by normal retirement age. After that you only need to earn enough to cover current costs. Try the Coast FIRE calculator.
What’s the difference between Lean FIRE and Fat FIRE?
Lean FIRE means retiring on a frugal budget (often under about $40,000 a year); Fat FIRE means a comfortable or generous budget (often $100,000 or more). The maths is identical – only the spending number changes.
Should I use real or nominal returns?
Use real returns (after inflation) and spending in today’s money, as the calculator does. Long-run real returns for a stock-heavy portfolio have historically been around 5–7% a year, but future returns may be lower, so a conservative assumption is wise.
How long will it take me to reach FIRE?
Mostly it depends on your savings rate. Starting from zero with a 5% real return, saving 50% of take-home pay reaches FIRE in about 17 years; saving 25% takes about 32 years. The calculator above shows your own timeline.
