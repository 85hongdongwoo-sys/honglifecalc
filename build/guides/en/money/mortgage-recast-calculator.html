Mortgage recast · lower payment, same loan

Is a mortgage recast worth it? How much a lump sum lowers your payment, what lenders charge, and recast vs refinance

Short answer: a recast re-spreads your remaining balance over the remaining term after you pay a lump sum. On a $400,000, 30-year loan at 6.5%, paying $50,000 after five years and recasting drops the payment from about $2,528 to $2,191 a month – roughly $338 less – while keeping your rate and payoff date.

Recasting (sometimes called re-amortization) is a quiet alternative to refinancing. You keep the same loan, the same interest rate and the same end date; the lender simply recalculates your monthly payment around a smaller balance. It’s popular with people who sell a previous home after buying a new one, or who receive an inheritance or bonus.

If saving the most interest is the goal, overpaying without recasting actually wins: your payment stays the same, so the loan ends years early. A recast is about cash flow – a lower required payment each month.

Who can recast?

  • Most conventional loans can be recast if the lender offers it; many require a minimum lump sum (commonly $5,000–$10,000) and a fee.
  • FHA, VA and USDA government-backed loans generally can’t be recast.
  • You’ll usually need a record of on-time payments.

Questions people ask about recasting

Does recasting lower my interest rate?
No. The rate stays exactly the same; only the balance and the monthly payment change. If rates have fallen a lot since you borrowed, a refinance may save more.
How much does a recast cost?
Lenders that offer it usually charge a flat administration fee, often in the low hundreds of dollars – far less than refinancing. Ask your servicer for its fee and minimum lump sum.
Does a recast affect my credit score?
Generally not – there’s no new loan application or hard credit check, unlike a refinance.
Is it better to recast or invest the lump sum?
Paying down a 6–7% mortgage is a guaranteed return at that rate. Investing may earn more over time but with risk. Keep an emergency fund either way, since money paid into a house is hard to get back out quickly.
