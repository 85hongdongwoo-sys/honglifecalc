Joules, calories and kilowatt-hours

Energy units explained: kcal vs kJ on food labels, kWh on your bill, and BTU for heating

Short answer: 1 kcal (one food “Calorie”) = 4.184 kJ. 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ = about 860 kcal = 3,412 BTU. So a 2,000 kcal daily diet holds about 8,370 kJ – roughly the energy of 2.3 kWh of electricity.

Energy is the same physical thing whether it comes from food, fuel or a power socket; only the units differ by tradition. Nutrition labels in Europe and Australia show both kJ and kcal, electricity bills use kilowatt-hours, and boilers and air conditioners in the US are rated in BTU. Converting puts them on one scale.

Two kinds of calorie

The converter offers both the thermochemical calorie (exactly 4.184 J, used for food labels) and the International Table calorie (4.1868 J, used in some engineering). The gap is under 0.1%, so it never matters for diet, but it can show up in the last digit of technical work.

Questions people ask

What’s the difference between kcal and Calories?
None. A food “Calorie” with a capital C is a kilocalorie. A label that says 250 Calories means 250 kcal, or about 1,046 kJ.
How do I convert kJ to kcal?
Divide by 4.184. A snack listed at 840 kJ is about 200 kcal.
Why can’t I “eat” 1 kWh of electricity worth of food and run a heater?
You can compare amounts, but the body turns food energy into work at only about 20–25% efficiency and the rest becomes heat. A person pedalling hard on a generator produces roughly 100–200 W.
How many BTU are in a kWh?
3,412.14 BTU. That’s why a 12,000 BTU-per-hour air conditioner moves about 3.5 kW of heat. For power ratings, use the power converter.
