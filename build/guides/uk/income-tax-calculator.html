How UK income tax works in 2026/27 – bands, the £100,000 trap and what changes in April 2027

Short answer: in England, Wales and Northern Ireland you pay nothing on the first £12,570, then 20% up to £50,270, 40% up to £125,140 and 45% above that. On a £60,000 salary that is £11,432 of income tax a year. The same bands apply in 2027/28 because the thresholds are frozen until 5 April 2031.

Income tax bands for 2026/27 (England, Wales and Northern Ireland)

Income tax is charged on your taxable income for the tax year that runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027. Taxable income means your salary, self-employed profit, pension income and rental profit added together, minus anything that is tax-free. The bands below assume you get the standard Personal Allowance.

Source: GOV.UK “Income Tax rates and Personal Allowances” and HM Treasury Budget 2026 rates annex. The basic rate band itself is £37,700 wide; it starts after your Personal Allowance.

The rates are marginal: moving into the higher rate band only taxes the pounds above £50,270 at 40%, not your whole salary. That is why your overall (effective) rate is always lower than your top rate.

How the calculator works it out, step by step

  1. Add up your income for the tax year – salary before tax, bonuses, taxable benefits, self-employed profit, pension and rental profit.
  2. Take off reliefs that reduce income: for example pension contributions made through net pay, or allowable business expenses.
  3. Work out your Personal Allowance. It is £12,570, but it falls by £1 for every £2 of adjusted net income over £100,000.
  4. Extend the basic rate band by the gross amount of any relief-at-source pension contributions and Gift Aid donations.
  5. Fill each band in order – 0%, then 20%, 40% and 45% – and add the tax from each slice.

The £100,000 “60% tax trap”

Once your adjusted net income goes over £100,000, you lose £1 of Personal Allowance for every £2 you earn above it. By £125,140 the allowance is gone. Each extra £100 in that range costs £40 in higher-rate tax plus another £20 because £50 of allowance disappears – an effective rate of 60%, before National Insurance.

Example: at £110,000 your allowance falls to £7,570, so income tax is £33,432 – £6,000 more than at £100,000 for £10,000 of extra pay. Paying more into a pension (including salary sacrifice) or making Gift Aid donations lowers adjusted net income and can bring back some or all of the allowance. If you live in Scotland

What changes from April 2027 (2027/28)

  • Main bands stay the same: Personal Allowance £12,570, basic rate limit £37,700 and additional rate threshold £125,140. The freeze runs until 5 April 2031.
  • Savings interest above your allowances will be taxed at 24% / 44% / 49% instead of 20% / 40% / 45%.
  • Property income (rental profit) gets its own rates of 24% / 44% / 49%.
  • Dividends stay at 10.75% / 35.75% / 39.35%, with a £500 dividend allowance.
  • The Personal Savings Allowance stays £1,000 for basic-rate and £500 for higher-rate taxpayers; the £5,000 starting rate for savings remains.
Because the thresholds are frozen while wages rise, more people move into the 40% band each year (“fiscal drag”). If a pay rise takes you just over £50,270, a slightly larger pension contribution can keep that slice at 20%.

Allowances that lower your bill

  • Marriage Allowance: a non-taxpayer can transfer £1,260 of allowance to a basic-rate spouse or civil partner, saving up to £252 a year. See the Marriage Allowance calculator.
  • Pension contributions: relief at source adds 20% automatically; higher-rate taxpayers claim the extra through Self Assessment or a tax code change.
  • Trading and property allowances: the first £1,000 of side-hustle income and £1,000 of property income are tax-free.
  • Blind Person’s Allowance: £3,250 in 2026/27, rising to £3,370 in 2027/28.

How the tax is collected

Employees and pensioners usually pay through PAYE: your employer uses your tax code (most commonly 1257L, meaning £12,570 of allowance) to deduct tax each payday. If you are self-employed, have rental or untaxed income, or need to claim higher-rate pension relief, you usually file a Self Assessment return. For 2026/27 the online return and any tax due are due by 31 January 2028 (paper returns by 31 October 2027). Check what your code means with the tax code calculator.

Questions people ask about UK income tax

How much income tax do I pay in the UK?
It depends on what you earn, but here’s the quick way to think about it: the first £12,570 is yours tax-free, the next £37,700 is taxed at 20%, and anything above £50,270 is taxed at 40%. So someone on £30,000 pays £3,486 a year, and someone on £40,000 pays £5,486. Pop your salary into the calculator above and it does the band-by-band maths for you.
How is income tax calculated in the UK?
Your income is cut into slices, and each slice has its own rate. Only the part that falls inside a band is taxed at that band’s rate – so getting into the 40% band never makes your whole salary 40%. The calculator follows exactly the steps shown above: allowance first, then 20%, 40% and 45%.
Is income tax going up in 2027?
The rates for wages aren’t changing in 2027/28 – they stay at 20%, 40% and 45%, and the £12,570 allowance stays frozen until April 2031. What does change from April 2027 is tax on savings interest and rental profit, which move to 24%, 44% and 49%. So if your income is mostly a salary, your tax bill on that salary won’t jump in April 2027.
Does income tax include National Insurance?
No – they’re two separate deductions, even though both appear on your payslip. Income tax funds general government spending; National Insurance (8% between £12,570 and £50,270 for employees) builds your entitlement to the State Pension and some benefits. Our take-home pay calculator shows both together.
Do I need to fill in a tax return?
Most employees don’t – PAYE takes the right amount from each payslip. You’ll usually need Self Assessment if you’re self-employed, earn rent above £1,000, have untaxed income, or want to claim extra relief on pension contributions as a higher-rate taxpayer. If you’re unsure, GOV.UK has a short checker that tells you in a couple of minutes.
Could I be owed a tax refund?
Quite possibly, especially if you started a job partway through the year, were put on an emergency tax code, or stopped working before April. HMRC usually sorts this out automatically after the tax year ends and sends a P800 letter. You can also check your Personal Tax Account online – it’s free, and you never need a paid agent to claim it.
Do pensioners pay income tax?
Yes, if your total income (State Pension plus any private or workplace pension) goes over £12,570. The State Pension is paid without tax taken off, so any tax due is usually collected through the tax code on your other pension.
