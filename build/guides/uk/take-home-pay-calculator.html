What you actually take home in 2026/27 – a payslip guide with monthly figures for common UK salaries

Short answer: on a £40,000 salary in England, Wales or Northern Ireland you keep about £2,693 a month (£32,320 a year) after £5,486 income tax and £2,194 National Insurance, with no pension or student loan. A 5% pension and a Plan 2 student loan bring that down to about £2,480–£2,510 a month, depending on the pension type.

Reading your payslip line by line

  1. Gross pay – your salary for the period, plus overtime, bonus or commission paid that month.
  2. Pension – if your scheme uses salary sacrifice or net pay, it is taken before tax; with relief at source it is taken from pay after tax and the provider adds 20%.
  3. Income tax (PAYE) – worked out with your tax code. 1257L gives £1,047.50 of tax-free pay each month.
  4. National Insurance – 8% of monthly earnings between about £1,048 and £4,189, then 2% above.
  5. Student loan – 9% of pay above your plan’s threshold (6% for a postgraduate loan), collected with tax.
  6. Net pay – what reaches your bank account.

Salary sacrifice vs relief at source: which leaves you more?

The pension type changes your take-home pay even for the same contribution. Take a £50,000 salary with a 5% (£2,500) pension contribution:

Pension typeTax savedNI savedCost to you of £2,500 in the pension
Salary sacrifice£500£200£1,800
Net pay arrangement£500£0£2,000
Relief at source£500 (added by provider)£0£2,000

Salary sacrifice also saves National Insurance, which is why it leaves you the most. From 6 April 2029 the National Insurance saving will only apply to the first £2,000 a year of salary-sacrificed pension contributions; the income tax saving is not affected.

Why your payslip may not match this calculator

  • Emergency tax code (1257L W1/M1, 0T or BR) after starting a new job – you get any overpaid tax back automatically once HMRC sends the right code.
  • Taxable benefits such as a company car or private medical insurance reduce your tax code, so more tax is taken.
  • Scotland: Scottish taxpayers have six income tax bands; on £40,000 the income tax is about £5,551 instead of £5,486.
  • Bonus months: PAYE treats a bonus as part of that month’s pay, so more of it can fall in the 40% band for that month.
  • Over £100,000: the Personal Allowance tapers away, creating an effective 60% rate up to £125,140. See the 60% tax trap calculator.
Thinking about a pay rise? Because bands are frozen, a rise that crosses £50,270 is taxed at 40% plus 2% National Insurance, so you keep 58p of each extra pound (49p if you also repay a Plan 2 loan). Check the effect with the income tax calculator.

Questions people ask about take-home pay

Is take-home pay gross or net?
Net. Gross pay is what your contract says before anything comes off; take-home pay is what actually lands in your bank account after income tax, National Insurance, pension contributions and any student loan. When a job advert says “£35,000”, that’s gross – you’d take home about £2,393 a month.
How much is £30,000 a year after tax?
Around £25,120 a year, or £2,093 a month, with no pension or student loan. That’s £3,486 in income tax and £1,394 in National Insurance.
What’s the take-home pay on £50,000?
About £39,520 a year – roughly £3,293 a month. £50,000 sits just under the £50,270 higher-rate threshold, so none of it is taxed at 40% yet.
How much is £100k after tax in the UK?
About £68,557 a year, or £5,713 a month, before pension or student loan. Right above £100,000 you start losing your Personal Allowance, so each extra £1 between £100,000 and £125,140 is effectively taxed at 60% – a well-placed pension contribution can make a big difference here.
What’s the take-home pay on minimum wage?
From April 2026 the National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour. Full-time at 37.5 hours a week, that’s about £24,785 a year, and you’d take home roughly £21,365 – around £1,780 a month. It rises to £13.21 an hour from April 2027.
Why is my take-home pay so low this month?
The usual culprits are an emergency tax code after a job change, a bonus pushing part of that month into a higher band, a new pension contribution, or student loan repayments starting. Check the tax code on your payslip first – if it ends in W1, M1 or X, or says 0T or BR, you may be overpaying temporarily, and HMRC will refund it once your code is corrected.
Does take-home pay include my pension?
No – take-home pay is after pension contributions are taken. The money still belongs to you, it’s just going into your pension pot instead of your current account. With salary sacrifice, it also saves you National Insurance, so your take-home pay drops by less than the amount you put in.
